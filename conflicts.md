# Five Percent conflict library

Seventy scenarios for the 7th Quadrant campaign. Each starts with a dilemma the crew can respond to. Consequences can carry into later missions and Wyoming debriefs.

## Signals and first contact

1. **The Last Ping**. A drifting survey beacon sends a maintenance ping using the call sign of a crew whose ship was declared lost. The battery can sustain one reply or one high-resolution direction fix, and attempting either may end the transmission.
2. **Borrowed Light**. A flash that looks like a distress signal repeats at exactly the interval of an old calibration lamp. A rescue pilot wants to break formation, while navigation asks for a second observation from a different angle.
3. **Tomorrow's Old News**. The radio receives a ten-year-old Earth broadcast with a fresh checksum and no relay record. The archivist wants a full copy, but the communications officer needs the channel for an approaching supply ship.
4. **The Quiet Window**. An independent observatory asks the Quadrant to switch off its transmitter for eleven minutes to measure a faint source. Those are the same eleven minutes Wyoming has allocated to reach a family waiting for word from a crewmate.
5. **A Voice on the Wrong Channel**. A dock authorization comes through in the right voice with a greeting local operators never use. The dockmaster wants to keep traffic moving, while the translator android asks for a challenge through a different channel.
6. **The Missing Minute**. Two intact logs disagree by a minute about when the drive went offline. Publishing either as the master clock would make the other crew's account look false, even though both instruments passed their checks.
7. **The Warning Arrives Late**. A buoy reports a debris crossing after the Quadrant has already passed the point. Its timestamp may be wrong, or a second group of debris may still be coming, and a turn would use the lander's reserved propellant.
8. **Two Distances**. A public ephemeris and the ship's star tracker place the same moon in different positions. The team can keep the scheduled flyby based on its own tracker or postpone until an independent station can settle the discrepancy.
9. **Weather with a Message**. A commercial weather bulletin appears to carry a deliberate extra pulse that could be an emergency signal. A merchant captain asks the crew to ignore the anomaly, saying decoding it may expose confidential route data.
10. **An Anonymous Correction**. Someone sends a precise correction that removes the noise in the observatory's latest scan. They decline to identify themselves or describe their instrument, leaving the crew to choose between an immediate result and a traceable process.

## Supply and survival

11. **The Last Filter**. The Quadrant and a hospital shuttle both need the same replaceable air filter. Each can postpone service for a short time, but the hospital cannot move patients quickly and the Quadrant is due for a long departure burn.
12. **The Cold Cargo**. A contracted freezer is holding research tissue and a crate of temperature-sensitive medicine. A failing cooler can keep only one at specification until docking, and neither cargo owner agreed to a shared risk.
13. **The Fuel Ledger**. An accounts correction gives the Quadrant a smaller fuel allotment after it has already promised to escort a slow freighter. The station can restore the fuel if the crew surrenders its next independent survey slot.
14. **The Borrowed Bay**. An independent mechanic lends the crew a repair bay for one night. A disabled passenger carrier then requests the same space, and moving the Quadrant back into the queue would cost its launch window.
15. **The Heavy Return**. A mineral sample may be the discovery of the year, but bringing it home means unloading the drums set aside for a struggling hydroponics crew. Returning with a small certified core would keep the promise and leave most of the find behind.
16. **Off-Brand Gaskets**. A local shop offers compatible seals at a fraction of the contracted price. They have no flight certificate, but the shop has records of years of successful use in stationary modules and the certified order is late.
17. **The Orange Crate**. The cargo robot reports that an emergency kit and a museum display have identical inventory codes. Loading the wrong crate could leave a survey party without its kit, but breaking the museum seal loses the exhibit's authenticity certification.
18. **The Water Tally**. A recycler improves the official water reserve while its waste tank grows lighter than the balance sheet expects. The engineer wants to isolate it, whereas the greenhouse needs the water cycle to stay online until the seedlings are moved.
19. **The Hotel Load**. A rescue detour has left the ship with half the battery margin expected for ordinary cabin services. The crew can shut down communal lighting and the galley or shorten the instrument session to keep shared areas usable.
20. **Spares for Tomorrow**. A younger crew wants their own emergency pump after seeing Wyoming's single spare in the rack. The quartermaster can release it, but removing it leaves the station's older pumps with no backup until the next shipment.

## Governance and ownership

21. **The First Claim**. Two groups file claims for the same resource field using different coordinate conventions. One is backed by a larger company, while the smaller consortium's dated observations may be the only basis for deciding which filing came first.
22. **The Shared Waiver**. UP asks the whole crew to sign one waiver before an experimental run. A technician wants the chance to opt out privately and is willing to share the extra shift with someone who knowingly volunteers.
23. **Who Owns the First Map?**. Wyoming funded the survey and wants first publication rights. The people who built the actual charts want a public release that includes their names and lets other stations check the results immediately.
24. **The Last Seat**. There is one unassigned seat on the next craft leaving Wyoming. A stranded trainee needs it to get home, while an incoming specialist needs the same flight to cover the night shift at an isolated station.
25. **Nobody Signed**. A request to open an old station module has approval marks from three offices but no identifiable signer. The station master can authorize the opening personally, or keep an investigation waiting until the owner can be located.
26. **The Midnight Contract**. A supplier changed its salvage terms after the crew had already entered the recovery zone. The new arrangement gives better pay now but gives the supplier ownership of anything the crew finds on future missions in the sector.
27. **A Blind Review**. The lab receives a strong but anonymous safety objection with enough detail to be credible. The flight director wants a named expert before postponing and the author says company policy forbids them from speaking publicly.
28. **Priority at the Gate**. A company paid for guaranteed docking priority, but the new docking algorithm moved it ahead of inbound craft that had already committed fuel. The station has time to advertise one priority rule before the next wave arrives.
29. **A Patch on Loan**. A software fix makes the pressure gauges easier to read, but the license bars crews from sharing it with other stations. The only common copy of the old software can be repaired if the engineer is given another shift.
30. **The Closed Test**. UP offers the Quadrant the first look at a new instrument if all measurements remain private for a year. Another group needs the same observations to challenge an inaccurate map already used by civilian pilots.

## SNP drive and navigation

31. **The Warm Bearing**. The engine gauge is within its allowed range, but one bearing has warmed on three consecutive legs. The manufacturer recommends continued monitoring while the chief engineer asks for an idle leg to confirm the trend.
32. **A Push with the Drive Off**. After shutdown, the ship's accelerometer records a small push that the crew did not feel. If the reading is real the published safe distance is wrong, but preserving the original configuration takes the ship out of rotation.
33. **Equal Numbers, Unequal Dose**. One dosimeter gives a safe average by combining a low reading with a sharp local peak. The medic wants to close the affected corridor, while engineering needs the shortest route to finish its checks.
34. **The Broken Seal**. A wax witness mark on the sealed drive casing has shifted without a matching maintenance entry. The technician wants to stop and document it, while the pilot argues that changes in temperature could have done the same thing.
35. **Calibration Debt**. A rushed service fitted a replacement mass sensor without its final calibration pass. The old correction factors can get the ship out of dock, but the lab would lose the reference conditions for its next experiment.
36. **One in Ten**. A transient fault occurs once in roughly ten cold starts and never when the engine is warm. The test pilot can request a string of cold starts to isolate it, but doing so will delay a departure with a narrow window.
37. **Competing Zeroes**. The research instrument and the navigation instrument both demand to be treated as the zero reference. Switching to either would make some previous measurements incomparable just as the mission is supposed to compare two flights.
38. **The Drive Echo**. A remote ship reports that the Quadrant's test pulse appeared on its instruments with an unexplained delay. Repeating the run could confirm the effect but would disturb an observatory whose next measurement is already scheduled.
39. **A Ration of Power**. During a brief voltage dip, the flight recorder and the environmental rack ask for the last stable supply circuit. The rack can be cooled passively, but only if the crew leaves a normally occupied cabin for an hour.
40. **A Dockside Drift**. The ship drifts a small distance while both the station and onboard systems insist that the docking clamps are engaged. The dockmaster recommends keeping the passage sealed, while the maintenance team wants to approach the joint for a close inspection.

## Ecology and field science

41. **A Bloom in the Dust**. A sample changes color only when exposed to the ship's own lamp. The biologist wants to test for contamination, while the sponsor is already drafting a release about possible alien life.
42. **Downwind of a Footprint**. The shortest landing route crosses a sheltered basin with no recorded human visits. The lander pilot can accept the route and its excellent margin or land farther away to reduce the chance of contaminating the basin.
43. **The Interrupted Ice Core**. A promising ice core will thaw unless it can be moved to a better freezer soon. A fragile microbial comparison sample already inhabits that freezer, and moving it risks losing the only reference for both results.
44. **The Beacon Above the Herd**. An observation drone's beacon matches a cycle in the movement of a herd-like pattern below. Turning it off may cost control of the drone, but keeping it on may be the reason the pattern is changing.
45. **Artificial Dawn**. Clouds in a public exoplanet visualization suggest rainfall even though only the radius and orbital period are measured. The outreach team likes the image, and a field scientist fears future visitors will mistake it for a forecast.
46. **The Sample Nobody Can Repeat**. A robotic collector returns a remarkable material but its position log was corrupted. The team can report it as an unlocated specimen or wait until another crew has had a chance to find it independently.
47. **Radiant Stress**. The crew finds a greenhouse strain surviving a strange light cycle better than the seed bank's standard crop. Wyoming wants cuttings immediately, but sharing it before its behavior is understood may endanger other growers' stock.
48. **The Rainless River**. A broad surface channel looks young even though the weather record has never shown rain. A quick flyby would favor one angle, while an extra orbital pass would test the possibility that the apparent channel is a shadow.
49. **The White Tile**. A pristine ceramic marker found at a remote site could be geological, manufactured, or a fragment of an earlier probe. Recovering it would remove a key piece of its surroundings, while leaving it exposes it to the next dust storm.
50. **Second Air**. A newly detected atmospheric component is harmless to the sampling robot but reacts with one batch of the human crew's filters. The robot can finish the survey alone or the humans can postpone it until a tested batch arrives.

## Crew and community

51. **The Double Shift**. A navigator agrees to cover a colleague's second shift after hearing about a family emergency on Earth. Fatigue becomes obvious during a routine rehearsal, and calling it out publicly would expose a private situation the colleague has not shared.
52. **The Name Under the Plot**. A senior UP researcher presents a calibration improvement that a junior technician helped discover. The technician wants their name added, but asks the captain not to turn a conversation about credit into a public humiliation.
53. **The Dinner Tab**. The cook has been giving extra meals to a visiting crew and charging them to their own allotment. The quartermaster says the ration ledger now makes the ship look wasteful, and the visitors are already counting on eating together tonight.
54. **Two Captains in a Room**. A visiting test pilot uses familiar shorthand in a briefing and the ship's own pilot treats it as an order. Both are competent, but only one can have the final word on the next approach.
55. **The Unsent Letter**. A damaged terminal makes a private personal message appear in a shared transmission queue. The communications officer can ask its author whether to recover it, or remove it quietly and leave the sender unaware that it was exposed.
56. **A Bed for the Guest**. A visitor recovering from a rough descent is asleep in the only cabin that isolates engine noise. A crew member needs the same space before the night watch, and neither has a safe alternate berth assigned.
57. **The Borrowed Tool**. A rare gauge has gone missing from the workshop as a new apprentice starts a shift. It may be a recording mistake, a quiet loan, or an actual theft, and the crew is already treating the apprentice as guilty.
58. **Camera in the Mess**. An automatic incident recorder also captured an argument in the mess hall. The investigation needs the first seconds, but including the rest would turn a private disagreement into a permanent crew record.
59. **The Exit Interview**. A valued engineer plans to leave after the next return to Wyoming and has not told their closest colleague. The captain needs a staffing plan now, and an early announcement could make the remaining flight unnecessarily tense.
60. **Which Home?**. Two crew members insist that the returning ship stop at different stations because people there depend on them. Neither stop is frivolous, and changing the flight plan for one would make the other several days late.

## Robots and androids

61. **The Robot Vote**. A maintenance android has heard everyone on both sides of a shift dispute and asks to be included in the vote. The charter never defined its status, and treating the request as irrelevant would change how coworkers trust it.
62. **The Restore Point**. A service android can accept a repair that rolls its memory back by three days. It asks the crew to first preserve an unofficial promise it made to a stranded traveler, although the repair depot wants it back immediately.
63. **The Robot That Stopped**. A cargo machine refuses a lift it was certified to carry because the crates are making a sound missing from its training data. The shift lead wants to override the refusal, while its technician wants to inspect the contents first.
64. **Many Owners of One Drone**. A rescue drone recognizes two valid operators whose commands are mutually exclusive. Revoking one credential will strand an otherwise capable team without control, while a shared handover breaks the planned rescue timetable.
65. **The Archive Gap**. The archivist android can fill a damaged log with extremely plausible predictions, but it cannot certify them as records. A review board wants a complete timeline and is prepared to forget which entries came from a model.
66. **Off Duty by Choice**. A guide android schedules an hour of its own time while the dock is unusually busy. The station wants to treat it as active equipment, while the android argues that its quiet hour is the first choice it has ever made about its day.
67. **A Borrowed Face**. A supplier installed a popular public face on a new reception android, but the matching real person has never agreed to it. The station can disable the visible face while arranging a replacement or keep it briefly to cover an emergency reception shift.
68. **Give Me a Reason**. A scheduling tool repeatedly routes the same workers to the least desirable berths. Its creator says the score is valid and cannot be explained quickly, but the roster shows a clear pattern that the workers can point to themselves.
69. **The Old Rule**. A legacy service unit still treats any visitor without a paper work order as an intruder. Replacing the old rule may remove useful protection for a restricted compartment, while doing nothing locks authorized digital visitors outside.
70. **The Good Summary**. An automated summary perfectly matches the official mood of a briefing and leaves out the awkward questions the crew asked. The recorder can publish the complete transcript or circulate a short version that includes the missing objections but names no speakers.
